# When a “Rumor” Predicts Future Policy: Six Clocks of Historical Evidence

*The Boundaries of the Sayable — English publication-candidate draft v0.3, October 30, 2026. Frozen for editorial review; not independently reviewed or published.*

What if a “rumor” is not a statement about an existing fact but a prediction about what the government is about to do?

During Shanghai's March 2022 COVID-19 outbreak, online accounts suggested that the city would shortly be locked down for seven days or entirely closed for four. Authorities publicly rejected the reports, and police announced investigations into two individuals. On March 27 the municipality announced a phased lockdown-and-testing program; on March 31 it introduced longer restrictions for some areas, and on April 4 it said citywide controls would continue while further testing and assessments were completed.

Neither “the rumor was true all along” nor “the original denial was perfectly vindicated” tells the whole story. Each collapses several distinct questions: *What exactly was claimed? What evidence existed then? What did the government announce? What coercive action followed? What changed afterwards?*

A historical inquiry needs at least six clocks.

<span class="sc-section-anchor" id="clock-1"></span>

## 1. The clock of the original utterance

The first version we can actually inspect may be a reporter's account of a circulating claim, not the original message.

A news report displayed at **23:21 on March 22** described a circulating claim that a State Council inspection visit would be followed by a seven-day Shanghai lockdown. It also reported that a Shanghai rumor-checking platform had consulted authoritative local departments and rejected the claim. This establishes that **media reporting of an authoritative denial was already public that evening**. It does **not** authenticate the original WeChat post, its first sender, or its creation time. The separate four-day account mentioned in a March 23 police statement must not be conflated with this seven-day source without the original messages.

[Contemporaneous March 22 report](https://news.sina.com.cn/o/2022-03-22/doc-imcwipih9980616.shtml).

<span class="sc-section-anchor" id="clock-2"></span>

## 2. The clock of what a statement predicts

“Shanghai will all shut down for seven days,” “the entire city will shut down for four days,” and “different parts of the city will face sequential four-day restrictions” are different propositions.

The March 27 official notice set out **planned** restrictions for Pudong, southern Shanghai and adjacent districts from **March 28, 05:00 to April 1, 05:00**, and for Puxi from **April 1, 03:00 to April 5, 03:00**. The stated phases therefore **overlapped by two hours**. The notice also said that Puxi's existing high-priority restricted areas would remain under controls during the first phase. This was a geographically phased plan, **not two strictly non-overlapping, citywide four-day blocks**. Later extensions changed implementation, but did not retroactively verify every detail of the earlier accounts.

Assess the **start date, geography, duration, degree of certainty, and purported source** of each precise claim before attempting to classify its accuracy.

[March 27 municipal notice](https://www.shanghai.gov.cn/sjzccs/20220327/613eda924f814a4ab4b25642f0e668c5.html).

<span class="sc-section-anchor" id="clock-3"></span>

## 3. The clock of official labeling

**A rebuttal attributed to authoritative municipal sources was already carried in the March 22, 23:21 press report.** Separately, at **08:10:40 on March 23**, Xinhua republished the Shanghai Government Information Office's Weibo statement rejecting circulating citywide-lockdown claims and describing grid-based screening. These are **different publication and attribution events**. March 23 must not be described as the first public denial. Official fact labels are assertions with unusual practical reach: they are distributed through authoritative channels by institutions that also possess privileged access to policy deliberations.

A denial of a future policy proposal might mean the proposal was unfounded, had not been decided, or was not the policy *at that moment*. It does not automatically promise that no later policy will resemble the prediction.

This distinction is not an argument that all official denials are false. It is an argument that public authorities should be precise about the evidence supporting them.

[March 23 official statement republished by Xinhua](https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2022-03/23/c_1128494505.htm).

<span class="sc-section-anchor" id="clock-4"></span>

## 4. The clock of enforcement

By **17:59:14 on March 23**, a Yicai republication of a Shangguan News police report stated that two people were under criminal investigation for allegedly fabricating and deliberately spreading false information. That publicly documents an **investigation announcement**. It does not independently establish a conviction, the subjects' motive, the actual degree of disruption, or the eventual disposition.

Chinese joint judicial guidance issued in February 2020 distinguished malicious fabrication from mistaken circulation that caused little harm. A subsequent Shanghai High Court advisory discussed the legal requirements for the offense. Whether specific elements were met in the March 2022 case cannot be determined without the original messages and case evidence.

[17:59:14 police announcement as reported contemporaneously](https://www.yicai.com/news/101358444.html) · [18:10 same-source police republication](https://sh.people.com.cn/n2/2022/0323/c134768-35188846.html) · [Joint judicial guidance](https://www.beijing.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengcefagui/202002/t20200211_1627613.html).

<span class="sc-section-anchor" id="clock-5"></span>

## 5. The clock of subsequent policy change

At 22:51 on March 31, Xinhua had already reported newly announced graded controls for parts of eastern Shanghai, including some 14-day or seven-plus-seven-day arrangements. This cannot be accurately described as information that first became public at the April 1 press conference.

On April 4 the city announced continued restrictions pending follow-up testing and assessment. Thus the March 27 four-day phases cannot serve as a complete retrospective account of what every resident experienced.

But a policy announced in April is not evidence that a person spreading a message in March had access to the eventual decision.

[March 31 report](https://www.xinhuanet.com/local/2022-03/31/c_1128521854.htm) · [April 4 continuation reported in April 5 print edition](https://paper.people.com.cn/hwbwap/html/2022-04/05/content_25910900.htm).

<span class="sc-section-anchor" id="clock-6"></span>

## 6. The clock of retrospective archive inspection

A reader comparing today's online Shanghai government transcript of the March 26 press conference with press reports published *on March 26* can observe a significant difference.

The extant government Q&A does not fully include Wu Fan's explanation that an indiscriminate three-, five-, or seven-day shutdown would affect national and international economic activity. Contemporary reporting from The Paper and other newsrooms contains that explanation.

This raises an important question about completeness and institutional recordkeeping. Yet today's government webpage has **not** been authenticated as byte-identical to its first edition in 2022. Without an archived edition or an audited recording, we cannot say whether the passage was omitted initially, edited later, or censored intentionally.

[Current government transcript](https://www.shanghai.gov.cn/nw9820/20220326/62e454513fbf4b37a48d9b9d769887ef.html) · [The Paper, March 26, 11:01](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_17315665) · [same-day CCTV reporter's account republished by CNStock](https://news.cnstock.com/news,yw-202203-4852153.htm).

## A comparison with a documented correction: Li Wenliang

The Li Wenliang case follows a different procedural path. On December 30, 2019, he shared an early warning about unexplained pneumonia among medical classmates. His description of seven cases as “SARS” was not an accurate identification of the emerging pathogen; a message later the same day referred to coronavirus infection and ongoing typing. Police questioned him and issued a written reprimand on January 3.

An essential correction to the evidentiary chronology: **Li's personal admonition was publicly discussed before March 19**. A January 31, 11:29 Beijing News interview recorded Li describing the January 3 written reprimand and saying he had not been detained. A later January 31 syndicated Upstream News interview published a photograph of the reprimand attributed to Li. These are early public interview and document-image records, not the detailed institutional investigation that emerged in March. The image contains potentially identifying document fields and should not be republished unredacted. [Beijing News](https://m.bjnews.com.cn/detail/158044099415716.html) · [Upstream News interview syndicated by The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_5716857).

The photograph contains an initial police allegation of serious disruption of social order. On March 19, commission investigators said Li had **no subjective intention to disrupt public order**. These refer to different evidentiary dimensions—an asserted consequence and subjective intent—so the comparison deserves scrutiny without being presented as a simple logical contradiction. [Commission Q&A](https://m.12371.gov.cn/content/2020-03/19/content_41680.html).

The two January 31 interviews also require careful contextual reading. Asked broadly about the reprimand's impact, Li said it had not affected him especially much. Asked about specific enforcement and workplace risks in another interview, he described fear of detention and possible later retaliation by his employer. These are responses to **different questions**, not a controlled psychological measure or proof of a population-wide chilling effect. [Beijing News interview](https://m.bjnews.com.cn/detail/158044099415716.html) · [Upstream News interview, syndicated by The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_5716857).

The records of **correction**, not just the initial warnings, are crucial. On March 19, 2020, the National Supervisory Commission publicly identified the reprimand as improper and its procedure as irregular. Wuhan police withdrew it, apologized to Li's family, and announced disciplinary measures against two responsible officers.

The language of the institution also changed. On January 1 police reporting characterized eight unnamed people in terms of unlawful conduct and enforcement. On January 29 police publicly described their actions as minor enough to merit education and criticism, without statutory warnings, fines or detention. These are observable differences in the public label; they do not establish that every individual had initially received a formal administrative penalty.

**The hour of the initial label also needs source criticism.** An extant Wuhan municipal archive page displays *January 1, 11:36*, even though its URL contains an April 2020 archive path. Contemporaneous reporting says the original police Weibo announcement appeared **in the afternoon**; a related The Paper page displays *18:53*. Thus 11:36 should remain an **archive-page timestamp**, not be treated without an authenticated original edition as the police's first public issuance time. [Municipal archive, retrospectively accessible](https://www.wuhan.gov.cn/hdjl/rdhy/202004/t20200419_1023462.shtml) · [The Paper, January 1, 18:53](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_5402905) · [January 29 clarification available by 14:45](https://china.caixin.com/2020-01-29/101509309.html).

Nor did March 19 offer one undifferentiated moment of “correction.” By 19:04, CLS had posted an early bulletin stating the supervisory recommendation, with a further abridged account appearing at 19:14; the fuller authorized report was published at 19:28; a Sina News page displayed the police's withdrawal and apology at **20:55** (the earliest located dated syndication in this register, **not** the authenticated initial time of the police Weibo post); and the disciplinary announcement about two officers was published at 22:09. A supervisory **recommendation**, a police **revocation**, and individual officers' **disciplinary measures** are three different institutional actions.

**Minute-level timestamps introduce another source-critical problem: editions can change.** An extant Jiemian republication displays March 19, 19:06 as its publication time but also lists a 19:58 update; its present fuller text cannot establish what the 19:06 edition already contained. A Beijing News account syndicated by Sina displays 19:12, but its initial body likewise needs edition-level authentication. The 19:04, 19:14, and 19:28 points above are **displayed publication timestamps in the registered sources**, not an exhaustive inventory of everything publicly available at each minute. We must not backdate later-edited text to a page's initial timestamp. [Jiemian republication](https://www.jiemian.com/article/4141272.html) · [Sina syndication](https://finance.sina.com.cn/wm/2020-03-19/doc-iimxxsth0311486.shtml). [19:04 bulletin](https://www.cls.cn/detail/461600) · [19:14 summary](https://www.cneb.gov.cn/2020/03/19/ARTI1584615924612215.shtml) · [19:28 investigation](https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2020-03/19/c_1125737457.htm) · [20:55 withdrawal syndication](https://news.sina.cn/gn/2020-03-19/detail-iimxyqwa1770303.d.html) · [21:47 same-statement republication](https://news.youth.cn/gn/202003/t20200319_12247566.htm) · [22:09 discipline](https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2020-03/19/c_1125737967.htm).

The earlier police announcement about “eight” information spreaders did not publish a complete individualized roster. The January 29 clarification that the eight received no statutory warnings, fines or detention does not erase Li's separate written reprimand. [Contemporaneous Xinhua report of the January 29 clarification](https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2020-01/29/c_1125510476.htm). Nor do March 19 retrospective findings become information that the public knew on January 3.

[Supervisory Commission report](https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2020-03/19/c_1125737457.htm) · [Police withdrawal and disciplinary notices](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_6600938).

The comparison does not show that every case of alleged misinformation has the same outcome. It shows why a study of institutional accountability must record the **identity of the correcting body, what it corrected, its reasons, and what concrete actions followed**.

## The politics of the clocks

A citizen estimating future risks and a public agency labeling information false do not operate with equal authority, access to information or coercive power. That imbalance does not make a citizen's prediction automatically accurate. It creates a stronger responsibility for an institution to preserve its reasons, specify the limits of its claims, justify enforcement through evidence, and make corrections traceable.

The purpose of critical history is not to replace one indisputable narrative with another. It is to keep visible **what could be known at each moment, who had the power to label that knowledge, and which decisions later had to be revised**.
