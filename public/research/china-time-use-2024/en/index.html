Do these two figures measure the same thing?

No. The two scopes count different sets of activities : scope A counts paid work only, scope B counts paid plus unpaid work. Nobody changed their behaviour when you switched; only the statistical boundary of what is counted as work changed. That is why “men work longer” and “women work longer” can both come from the same data — as long as each assumes a different scope.

“Paid + unpaid” on this page is not all of a person's waking day. Personal necessities, education and training, transport and the rest have their own categories, and the six domains are mutually exclusive; internet use is a cross-cutting measure that overlaps them and cannot be added into 24 hours.

Reference: the six activity domains of a national average day (figures published in bulletin no. 2)

National population averages published in bulletin no. 2 (minutes per day; the six domains are mutually exclusive and sum to 1440 minutes, the gap to 1,440 coming from published rounding). Internet use (337 min) is a cross-cutting measure that overlaps those domains and is never added into 24 hours. This is not a by-sex figure and not the comparison this page analyses — it is here only to show how much room the two work categories take in a day.

Why does the ranking change?

The finding in one sentence:The reversal needs no model, only addition: the paid gap −73.9 plus the unpaid gap +99.8, giving a combined +25.9 .

When you read this chart, remember: This is the result of a change in statistical coverage . It does not imply any causal trade-off in which unpaid work displaces paid work, and it does not describe anyone converting work hours into housework hours during a day.

What does the official definition of unpaid work include?

It is not a synonym for “housework”. Below is the official footnote from bulletin no. 2, verbatim:

[8]无酬劳动领域包括家务劳动、陪伴照料家人、购买商品或服务等活动大类以及劳动就业活动大类中的无酬实习等就业活动、社会交往大类中的公益志愿活动。— 公报第二号（全国居民分领域/分大类时间利用）, original textFootnote [8]: the unpaid-work domain covers the activity categories of housework, accompanying and caring for family members, and buying goods or services; it also covers unpaid internship and similar employment activities within the employment activity category, and voluntary work within the social interaction category.The official bulletins exist in Chinese only. For contrast, the official paid-work definition excludes unpaid internships —

[7]有酬劳动领域包括劳动就业活动大类中除无酬实习等就业活动外的其他活动类别。— 公报第二号（全国居民分领域/分大类时间利用）, original textFootnote [7]: the paid-work domain covers all categories within the employment activity category except unpaid internship and similar employment activities.The official bulletins exist in Chinese only. The English lines below are our translation of the quoted official footnotes, not an official English version.

Both footnotes belong to one classification system, so the two scopes do not overlap ; adding them is a true sum of the two categories (no minute is counted twice).

Why does this distinction matter?

Two claims common in public discussion both overreach the data: reducing unpaid work to “housework”, or expanding it to “everything women carry”. The official categories cover housework and care, but also shopping, unpaid internships and voluntary work — experiences of very different intensity — and the diary records duration only: not how tiring it was, whether it was chosen, or whether it was interrupted.

Note also: bulletin no. 3 states that in the field work no resident aged 6–15 reported any activity in the paid-work domain, so the national averages for ages 6 and over used here include children and adolescents who do no paid work.

Each component has its own denominator: the participation rate is taken over all residents aged 6 and over of that sex, while the participant duration is taken over only those who did this kind of activity that day. Only their product is the population average — reading participant duration as a population average is the most common misreading.

Advanced: writing the difference as a two-term identity

Δm = (p♀ − p♂) × (t♀ + t♂)/2 + (t♀ − t♂) × (p♀ + p♂)/2

This is a fully symmetric arithmetic identity : the two terms sum exactly to the population-average difference Δm, whatever the order of expansion. It allocates the difference across “fewer/more people participating” and “participants spending longer/shorter”, but it does not mean “the participation rate caused this many minutes and the duration caused that many”.

The dashed line is only this activity's original difference (−73.9 min/day), drawn across the two decomposition rows; it is not an error bar and not an uncertainty interval. The displayed terms sum to −73.8, which differs from the displayed difference −73.9 by 0.1 minutes purely through rounding; the unrounded identity closes exactly.

01

Social Role Theory

Social Role Theory of Sex Differences

Social roles, family structure, opportunities and norms may be related to how work is divided; these data show the outcome of that division, but cannot test the mechanisms.

Article type: Encyclopaedia entryexternal theorysupplies a testable hypothesisrole beliefs not measured
The framework: what the theory proposes

What the theory proposes

This framework traces sex-related differences in behaviour back to the positions created by the division of labour : people who carry household and care responsibilities are expected to develop skills of caring and coordination, people engaged in paid work are expected to develop instrumental skills, and those expectations in turn shape how time is allocated. It describes the consequences of positions and expectations, not a fixed nature.

The prediction it makes testable

If the difference comes mainly from roles and opportunities, it should vary as the distribution of roles varies — larger or smaller across regions, periods, family structures or employment states; if it does not move with those conditions, the framework's explanatory claim is weakened. That is how it can be disconfirmed.

What kind of source this is

The page cites a short encyclopaedia entry (3 pages) describing the framework itself. No effect size is quoted, and none of the framework's empirical findings are transplanted onto the Chinese sample.

What this survey actually measured

The outcome of the division of labour: participation rates and participant durations in the two activity domains, and the population-average difference derived from them.

What is observable here is the division of outcomes: paid work women − men −73.9, unpaid work +99.8 min/day.

What these data cannot yet show

The bulletin measured no role beliefs, role expectations, intra-household bargaining or opportunity structure, and it does not jointly control age, employment, marital and childbearing status or household composition. So it cannot be determined whether the observed difference arises from a role mechanism — the theory must not be read as an identified causal explanation .

What a test would require

One would need role attitudes and actual care/employment arrangements measured in the same population, and an association with time allocation estimated after controlling for population composition; or a comparison across groups and periods whose role distributions differ markedly, to see whether the gap moves with them. None of that is in the published tables.

02

The cognitive dimension of household labour

The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor

Housework also contains anticipating, planning, deciding and monitoring components; an activity diary records which activity lasted how long, and does not necessarily capture who was carrying the worry.

Article type: Journal articleexternal theoryinterviews with 35 couples · not this surveycognitive dimension not measured
The framework: what the theory proposes

What the theory proposes

This research proposes that cognitive labour is not another activity category alongside housework, but a dimension of household labour — behind a single meal, medical visit or school run there are the phases of anticipating needs, finding options, deciding and monitoring progress. It is often invisible to both the person doing it and their partner, which is one reason it becomes a source of conflict.

What the existing study found

The original study is based on 70 in-depth interviews with 35 couples in US dual-earner households, and reports that the overall amount of cognitive labour and its anticipating and monitoring phases fell more often on women, while participation in the deciding phase was broadly similar between the sexes. That is a finding about that sample, not a result of this Chinese time-use survey.

What this survey actually measured

The diary records the duration of the activity in progress, in 15-minute units. The unpaid-work domain covers housework, accompanying and caring for family members, and buying goods or services, but contains no category corresponding to thinking about, planning or keeping track of.

Activity diaries record observable durations: unpaid work averages 175.4 min among women and 75.6 min among men, with no category corresponding to its cognitive dimension.

What these data cannot yet show

The amount of cognitive labour cannot be estimated, nor can any share of women's extra minutes be attributed to this dimension; equally, its absence from the diary does not license a conclusion that it does not exist or does not matter.

What a test would require

A test would need measurable instruments for cognitive and managerial responsibility (task inventories, decision responsibility, psychological-load scales) administered alongside the diary in the same comparable population, before one could examine whether it falls systematically on one sex.

03

Time poverty and temporal autonomy

Why time poverty matters for individuals, organisations and nations

“How much discretionary time you have” and “whether you feel short of time, or can decide how to use it” are not the same thing; these data speak only to the first.

Article type: Perspectivesubjective time feelings not measured
The framework: what the article proposes

What the article is about

Giurge, Whillans and West (2020) wrote this article in Nature Human Behaviour . The article is a Perspective (viewpoint article), which discusses time poverty as a question that matters for individuals, organisations and nations, and separates objective time scarcity from the subjective feeling of having no time. It is a viewpoint article, not an empirical analysis of China's 2024 survey; the article-type label is generated from the publisher's own page.

How far this page uses it

This page uses it only to pose a measurement distinction: objective duration and felt time affluence are not the same construct. The article's specific arguments and conclusions have not been checked word by word within this pack, so no effect, datum or policy recommendation of theirs is quoted here.

What this survey actually measured

Only the objective side: duration in the personal discretionary-activities domain (sport and fitness, cultural and leisure entertainment, and similar).

Objective discretionary time: women − men −15.7 min/day; there is no corresponding psychometric column.

What these data cannot yet show

The bulletin has no item on felt time affluence, time pressure or sense of control, and activity categories cannot separate voluntary from involuntary. So the discretionary-time difference must not be read as a difference in autonomy.

What a test would require

One would need an objective time diary and a subjective time-feelings scale administered to the same sample, to test whether the amount of time and the feeling about it agree, and which living conditions each is associated with.

The same survey also shows that women's average time in discretionary activities is lower than men's by 15.7 min/day (men 211.8 / women 196.1 ).

This is not “ 15.7 less autonomy”. The official categories are defined by activity content. Watching video may be a choice, or it may be all that other conditions allow; minutes of activity cannot tell the two apart, and cannot support a claim that the gap is caused by unpaid work. Discretionary time is only one of the mutually exclusive domains of a day; the rest goes to personal necessities, education and training, transport and so on.

What can this result support?

In one sentence:Unadjusted population averages from the May 2024 field work among usual residents aged 6 and over nationwide (weekdays weighted 5/7, rest days 2/7): paid work women − men −73.9 min/day, unpaid work +99.8 min/day, both categories combined +25.9 min/day.

✓What it does support

  • Under the official activity classification, the population-average time of residents aged 6 and over differs by sex, and the direction of the difference changes with the definition of what counts as work.
  • The direction of the combined gap is robust to the rounding of the published figures: it stays positive (women more) within the rounding-only bounds [+24.0, +27.7 ].
  • The difference coincides with women's higher participation in unpaid activity and their longer recorded duration among participants.
  • Anyone can recompute the same result from the formulas and published figures given on this page.

✕What it does not support

  • “Every woman works +25.9 minutes more than every man” — this is a group mean, not an individual distribution, a paired comparison or a universal statement.
  • “Sex itself causes the difference” — there is no joint distribution over age, employment, marital status, region and household composition, and no causal identification design.
  • “Women carry more of life overall” — study, transport, the intensity of care, multitasking and subjective pressure are all outside this sum.
  • “Discretionary time equals freedom, wellbeing or autonomy” — the official categories are not a psychometric.
  • Significance, trends across years, or cross-classified groups such as “young urban women” — the published tables give neither reportable sampling variance nor micro-level joint data.

Scope, weighting and what is not controlled
  • Field period: 2024-05-11/2024-05-31 — a single cross-sectional survey, not a year-round diary panel.
  • Population: 6 years and older, usual residents of sampled households; sample of 38,500 households / 107,000 people (figures rounded in the bulletin).
  • Recording: one weekday and one rest day per person (one weekday and one weekend day per respondent), in 15-minute units.
  • Weighting: a seven-day week, weekday 5/7, weekend 2/7.
  • Not controlled for: differences in population composition such as age, employment status, marital and childbearing status, household structure; every figure here is an unadjusted marginal average.
  • Not available: no individual micro-data, and the published tables provide no standard errors or reportable sampling confidence intervals.
  • Not cross-classifiable: sex × urban/rural × age cells cannot be derived from separate marginal tables.
  • Not comparable over time: the 2008 and 2018 surveys differ in coverage and classification, so this page makes no trend claim.
