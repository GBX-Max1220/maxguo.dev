# Who is working? How the gender gap in Chinese residents' time changes once unpaid work is counted

**China Residents' Time Budget Study · 2024 · descriptive study V1 · research report**\
**Population studied**: marginal (unadjusted) aggregates by sex for usual residents aged 6 and over in the 2024 Third Survey on Residents' Time Use.\
**Central finding**: counting paid work only, men's population-average time is about 74 minutes per day higher than women's; once unpaid work is added, women's combined total across the two categories is about 26 minutes per day higher than men's. This is a description of published statistical categories and marginal group differences. It is neither a complete measure of "all the work people do" nor a causal estimate of a gender effect.

## Abstract

When people ask "who works longer each day", they usually assume that work means paid labour. Time-use surveys also record activities that never enter wage statistics yet still occupy the day. This study uses the published summary tables of the National Bureau of Statistics' *Third Survey on Residents' Time Use* (bulletins no. 2 and no. 3) to compare the one-week weighted daily average time of Chinese residents aged 6 and over, by sex, in paid work, unpaid work and personal discretionary activities. Approximate population averages are reconstructed from participation rates and average time among participants, and the between-group difference is decomposed with a fully symmetric two-term identity. Within the published precision, women's paid work is 73.9 minutes per day lower than men's, their unpaid work 99.8 minutes higher, and the two categories combined 25.9 minutes higher; women's personal discretionary activities are 15.7 minutes lower. The unpaid-work difference resolves into the two arithmetic components of participation and participant duration, not into an identified causal mechanism. Because only uncrossed group aggregates are available, age, employment, marital and childbearing status, household structure and other compositional factors cannot be ruled out, and sampling error cannot be estimated. The conclusion is limited to **descriptive gendered time differences under different definitions of work**.

## 1. The key question: what exactly do we count as work?

"Men work longer" and "women work longer" look contradictory, yet may simply measure different things. Looking only at paid work restricts the statistics to activity related to wages and business income; adding unpaid work brings in housework, care, buying goods or services, and some unpaid internships and voluntary activity. The first scope is closer to time in directly remunerated activity; the second is closer to the total time the two domains take up in everyday life. Neither is "everything one has to do", and neither is a direct indicator of the intensity of labour, of experience, or of quality of life.

This study asks only one question that the available summary tables can answer: **once unpaid work is counted together with paid work, does the direction or size of the difference between men's and women's population averages change, and how does that change appear in participation rates and in average time among participants?**

## 2. Data, population and computation

The National Bureau of Statistics fielded the Third Survey on Residents' Time Use from 11 to 31 May 2024 among usual residents aged 6 and over in the sampled households, covering about 38,500 households and 107,000 people. Each respondent recorded one weekday and one rest day in 15-minute units. The published "daily average over a seven-day week" indicators are weighted 5/7 for weekdays and 2/7 for rest days. This is not a continuous diary of the whole year of 2024. See bulletin no. 1 and the notes to bulletin no. 2.

For a given activity, let `p` be the participation rate (0—1) and `t` the average daily duration among participants; the published group statistics then give:

> approximate population-average minutes per day `m = p × t`.

The group bulletin publishes `t` only to whole minutes and `p` only to 0.1 percentage points, so `m` is a product of rounded figures, not a new precise micro-level estimate. The male and female values are unadjusted averages for **all residents aged 6 and over**; they must not be written as "male employees" or "female workers", and they must not be called an age-standardised net effect of gender.

**Official domain definitions**: paid work covers the relevant employment activities but excludes unpaid internships and similar; unpaid work covers housework, accompanying and caring for family members, and buying goods or services, together with unpaid internships, voluntary and community work and the like; personal discretionary activities cover sport and fitness, cultural and leisure entertainment and similar, and are not the subjective feeling of "autonomy over one's time". Transport is a separate domain and includes more than commuting.

## 3. Result one: the male-female ranking reverses between scopes

| Indicator (min/day; approximate population average) | Men | Women | Women − men |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Paid work | 243.2 | 169.3 | −73.9 |
| Unpaid work | 75.6 | 175.4 | +99.8 |
| **Both categories combined** | **318.8** | **344.7** | **+25.9** |
| Personal discretionary activities | 211.8 | 196.1 | −15.7 |

Read only the first row and men do more paid work per day on average. Include the second row and the combined difference turns in women's favour. The phenomenon at issue is that **the measurement boundary changes, and the ordering of the groups changes with it**. This does not show which scope is uniquely correct; it reminds researchers and the public that any statement about "who works longer" must first say which activities it counts.

Note also that women's average personal discretionary time is about 16 minutes lower. But the difference between the two work categories cannot be treated as the cause of the discretionary gap: the whole 24 hours is also allocated to personal necessities, education and training, transport and the rest, and differences across domains cannot be causally attributed on this basis.

## 4. Result two: participation and participant duration jointly constitute the difference

The official participation rates and participant averages rule out a common misreading: reporting "how many hours participants worked" as though it were "how many hours all residents work on average".

| Activity | Men's participation | Women's participation | Men's participant duration | Women's participant duration |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Paid work | 60.2% | 47.7% | 404 min | 355 min |
| Unpaid work | 67.5% | 83.9% | 112 min | 209 min |
| Personal discretionary activities | 97.6% | 97.1% | 217 min | 202 min |

For the between-group difference `Δm = p♀×t♀ − p♂×t♂`, use the following **fully symmetric and exact arithmetic identity**:

`Δm = (p♀ − p♂)×(t♀+t♂)/2 + (t♀−t♂)×(p♀+p♂)/2`

| Activity (women − men, min/day) | Participation term | Duration term | Sum |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Paid work | −47.4 | −26.4 | −73.9 |
| Unpaid work | +26.3 | +73.4 | +99.8 |
| Personal discretionary activities | −1.0 | −14.6 | −15.7 |

> **Rounding note**: terms and totals are computed from unrounded values, so the displayed terms can sum to 0.1 minute away from the displayed total.
>

The gender difference in unpaid work therefore corresponds at once to a higher female participation rate and to longer recorded durations among those who participate. **These "terms" are differences allocated by the identity for `p×t`; they are not causal contributions in the sense of "the participation rate caused this many minutes and the duration caused that many".** The decomposition does not remove the compositional differences between the male and female groups and does not identify the mechanism that produces them.

## 5. What cannot be concluded from these data?

**First, not "every woman works 26 minutes more than every man".** This is a group mean, not an individual distribution, a paired comparison or a universal proposition.

**Second, not "sex itself causes a 26-minute difference".** There is no joint distribution over age, employment, marital and childbearing status, region and household composition, no individual-level data, and no causal identification design.

**Third, not "women's total life burden is exactly 26 minutes heavier".** Study, transport, subjective pressure in care, multitasking and the intensity of activity are not captured by this sum, and the official unpaid-work domain includes activities that not everyone experiences as a burden.

**Fourth, the official "discretionary activities" category cannot be renamed "freedom", "happiness" or "autonomy".** Watching television, for instance, may be a choice or may be all that other living conditions allow; the activity category itself cannot distinguish the two.

**Fifth, no conclusion about statistical significance, trends across years, or cross-classified groups.** The relevant sampling variance and micro-level joint data are not published; 2024 is a single cross-sectional point, and coverage and classification differ from the 2008 and 2018 surveys.

## 6. Conclusion: discuss the measurement boundary before arguing about the result

The most robust finding of this study is not a grand social explanation but a directly recomputable fact: **the 2024 published marginal tables of the National Bureau of Statistics show that, once unpaid work is counted, the ordering of men's and women's combined daily work time reverses relative to the paid-work-only comparison.** The difference requires no complex model, and even allowing for the rounding of the published indicators the direction of the combined difference remains women-higher.

This fact is worth discussing, but it does not by itself establish the institutional or household mechanisms behind it. Asking "why", "what policy follows", or "how much autonomous time a person really has" requires new joint data, an identifiable design, and different measurement constructs.

For argument with public data, the most valuable habit may be this: **before asking whether a conclusion is correct, ask what it measures, what it leaves out, and what evidence would be enough to change our judgement.**
