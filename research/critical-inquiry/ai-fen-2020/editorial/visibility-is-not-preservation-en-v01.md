# An Interview Can Survive—and Still Be Kept Out of Public View

**Ai Fen, the March 2020 “whistle” interview, and four clocks of editorial visibility · Unpublished research draft, 29 October 2026**

> RELEASE HOLD. The alleged editorial directives below were disclosed and described as verified by China Digital Times (CDT). We have not inspected the original transmission orders or their recipient logs. No independent second-reader approval.

On March 10, 2020, Chinese magazine *Renwu* published a reported interview with Ai Fen, a physician at Wuhan Central Hospital who described sharing early information about a pneumonia threat and the consequences she faced within her hospital. The political issue was not only whether a diagnostic report circulated. It was who could label an early warning, whose testimony would reach readers, and who could restrict that reach.

Surviving copies make a more precise account possible. They also require us to distinguish **a readable article**, **prominence on a homepage**, **a title chosen by an editor**, and **an original platform post**. None is interchangeable with the others.

## 1 | The person who “handed out the whistle”

A Terminus2049 Git version of the interview is recorded under the title *发哨子的人* (“the person who handed out the whistle”) in a March 10 commit with a **13:23:09 Beijing-time** committer timestamp. A same-day downstream version on a hospital-humanities site, preserved by a Wayback capture at **21:50:52 Beijing time**, instead used *吹哨子的人* (“the person who blew the whistle”) as its headline.

This change matters because the interview itself retains Ai Fen’s distinction between those two roles: conveying a warning is not necessarily the same act as publicly proclaiming it. Readers arriving through different titles first encounter a different framing of what she did.

Yet neither copy authenticates the initial WeChat edition of *Renwu*. We cannot establish who wrote the later title, why they chose it, or whether political instructions caused the change. [Earlier Git commit](https://github.com/Terminus2049/Terminus2049.github.io/commit/4dee83a23f66c4a2d7f4433b8e0ce5381101632b) · [Wayback capture](https://web.archive.org/web/20200310135052/http://www.yyrw.org.cn/e/action/ShowInfo.php?classid=5&id=2256).

## 2 | Removing an article from the homepage is not deleting its URL

A more consequential source emerged from [CDT’s December 11, 2020 disclosure](https://chinadigitaltimes.net/2020/12/minitrue-diary-march-10-2020-tibet-independence-covid-rent-relief-whistleblower-epidemic-transmission-and-treatments/). CDT reported acquiring and verifying a collection of propaganda directives said to have circulated in early 2020. One attributed to March 10 instructed editors not to feature coverage of the “whistle provider” on homepages, and to remove such coverage from a homepage if present.

The [Chinese record](https://chinadigitaltimes.net/chinese/655981.html) lists the **exact hospital-humanities article URL** that we separately identified in the historic Wayback record. This is a concrete connection between a purported editorial restriction and a specific surviving publication, not simply an assumption drawn from a broad climate of censorship.

The action described, however, is **homepage demotion**. It is not proof that the article’s own URL was disabled, that the site complied, or that the government demanded erasure of every mirror. A preserved article and instructions to keep such material off high-visibility pages can coexist. **Visibility is not a binary variable.** Searchability, homepage prominence, URL accessibility, and retention across archives are distinct dimensions.

## 3 | Naming is also subject to control

CDT’s later publication of a [LeTV internal-moderation corpus](https://chinadigitaltimes.net/chinese/728404.html) includes an entry purportedly dated February 7, 2020 that advised avoiding the designation *吹哨的人* in reporting. The March 10 entry concerns homepage prominence for reporting about a *发哨人*.

These should not be collapsed into a single “ban.” One is a reported **wording constraint**; the other, a reported **placement restriction**. We have no evidence that a particular editor changed the March interview title to follow either one. Indeed, the observed direction of the title change cannot be explained mechanically by the February wording instruction.

Their relevance is more specific: authorities or platform managers, as described by the leaked documents, treated both **the label for a person** and **the visibility of their story** as matters for editorial governance. This makes both appropriate subjects for a critical and falsifiable inquiry.

## 4 | The hidden-information clock

The stated issue date of an internal instruction is not the date the general public could consult it.

The March 10 restriction appears in a dated public CDT article from **December 11, 2020**. The LeTV documents were systematically published in **June 2026**, and the archived March 10 wiki item shows an import revision dated June 17, 2026. None of those retrospective public records can be inserted into what ordinary readers could verify on March 10, 2020.

Insiders who might receive directions and members of the public attempting to understand missing or deprioritized reporting occupy different information environments. That epistemic asymmetry is itself a mechanism of power.

A public archive provides a candidate original WeChat URL for the interview, `https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/nEy0jq5wjj-g6DQVZep1Wg`, and a candidate `archive.vn/OLdHs` capture. We have not authenticated the publisher’s first-edition bytes, a precise removal minute, or any original directive ordering the WeChat post itself to be removed. [nCovMemory CSV](https://github.com/2019ncovmemory/nCovMemory/blob/master/data/data.csv).

## 5 | Preserve the record without manufacturing certainty

In a downstream saved copy, an editorial sentence characterizing “eight admonished doctors” occurs twice, while Ai Fen’s first-person distinction about not having received a police admonition appears once. Text frequency is not the same as independent testimony. The January 2020 police notices about eight unnamed people and the later formal withdrawal of Li Wenliang’s individual written admonition must also be treated as distinct evidentiary and institutional acts.

Demanding better provenance for those claims should not be confused with treating censorship as harmless or with dismissing the testimony of medical staff. Precisely because institutions can classify speech and alter its distribution, they bear an especially strong responsibility to preserve original decisions, explain their grounds, and make subsequent correction possible.

The question is not merely whether people managed to copy an article. It is whether the public can retain **the warning**, **the evidence of restrictions on its visibility**, and **the ability to challenge the labels imposed on those who spoke**.
